Self-Directed PR Exercise · Fictional Scenario

PR Media Pitch & Story Angle

This sample shows how I would turn a hypothetical consumer-tech brief into a focused story angle, identify relevant media and creator audiences, write a personalized pitch, plan a follow-up, and define useful reporting metrics.

Portfolio transparency: This is an independent practice exercise. The brand, product, product features, and scenario below are fictional. No pitch has been sent, no journalist or creator has been contacted, and no campaign results or client work are being claimed.

1. The Hypothetical Brief

Fictional brand

Keyline Home

A made-up consumer-tech startup preparing to introduce a renter-oriented smart-home starter kit around CES 2027.

Concept product

Keyline Kit

For the purposes of this exercise, the concept includes removable sensors, plug-in controls, and a portable hub intended to avoid permanent installation.

Hypothetical communications goal: Earn relevant preview, review, or feature consideration by giving journalists and creators a useful story—not simply announcing that another product will appear at CES.

2. The Story Angle

What if smart-home technology were designed for people who rent—and need their setup to move with them?

A generic product announcement would lead with features and show presence. This angle starts with a practical constraint: some people cannot drill into walls, change fixtures, or leave equipment behind when they move. The product becomes an entry point to a larger consumer question about making connected-home technology work in temporary or small-space living.

Strategic hypothesis to validate: A renter-first use case may be more distinctive and immediately relatable to selected audiences than a broad “new smart-home product at CES” message. Before real outreach, I would validate the product claims, review recent coverage, and seek evidence from relevant users rather than present this hypothesis as an established market finding.

3. Audience and Pitch Adaptation

Consumer-tech and CES preview reporters

Lead with: The practical problem the concept solves and why it is worth a hands-on look.

Offer: A product walkthrough, clear specifications, and an interview with the fictional product designer.

Smart-home reviewers

Lead with: Set-up, compatibility, everyday utility, privacy details, and limitations that a reviewer can independently assess.

Offer: A structured demo and a complete feature/compatibility sheet. Any claims would need verification first.

Home, apartment-living, and design creators

Lead with: Making a space feel smarter without making permanent changes.

Offer: A visual set-up and take-down demonstration, with enough time for the creator to test it independently.

How I would build the list

Review recent bylines, product tests, CES previews, and creator videos; record topic fit, audience, format, geographic relevance, and contact source. Prioritize demonstrated interest rather than relying on a broad technology label alone.

4. Sample Journalist Pitch

The pitch below is a practice draft, written for a hypothetical reporter who covers consumer technology and CES previews.

Subject: CES 2027 preview: Smart-home tech for people who can't drill into their walls

Hi [First Name],

CES previews often focus on what connected devices can do next. I thought a different angle might fit your coverage: what does a smart-home setup look like for people who rent and cannot make permanent changes to their space?

For this hypothetical CES 2027 launch, Keyline Home is introducing the Keyline Kit, a renter-oriented smart-home concept built around removable sensors, plug-in controls, and a portable hub. The story would focus on the real-world experience—setup, everyday use, and what a user needs to consider before choosing connected devices for a rental.

We could arrange a hands-on walkthrough and an interview with the product designer, alongside a concise spec sheet for independent review. Would a renter-first smart-home preview be relevant to your CES plans?

Best,
[Your Name]

Why this pitch works: It leads with a reader-relevant question, makes the proposed angle clear early, keeps product description brief, and offers access that helps the recipient evaluate the idea. The opening should be personalized to the journalist's actual recent coverage before any real outreach.

5. Alternative Angles for Different Audiences

Hands-on review

Can a smart-home setup work without permanent installation?

Invite a reviewer to test set-up, usability, compatibility, and take-down rather than relying on marketing claims.

Home and design

Connected-home upgrades for spaces you don't own

Focus on the tension between personalizing a space and leaving it unchanged, with practical visuals.

Creator demonstration

A renter's smart-home set-up and reset

Build a short, transparent demonstration around setup and removal, noting any limits that testing reveals.

6. Follow-Up Example

Hi [First Name],

Following up on the renter-first smart-home idea for your CES planning. The demo could focus on the full user journey—from choosing a setup for a rental to removing it when moving—rather than a feature-by-feature product tour. If that fits your coverage, I can share the brief and arrange a time to walk through the concept.

Best,
[Your Name]

In a real campaign, I would follow the agency's timing and the journalist's stated preferences, make the follow-up useful, and stop if the recipient declines or asks not to be contacted.

7. Research and Verification Before Outreach

  • Validate every product claim: Confirm exact features, compatibility, installation requirements, data handling, availability, pricing, and what a demo can genuinely prove.
  • Review current coverage: Read recent stories from each proposed recipient to learn what they have covered, what is already familiar, and where a new angle might fit.
  • Test the audience insight: Support renter-related claims with reliable published research, interviews, or other evidence. Do not turn an appealing idea into a factual claim without support.
  • Prepare usable assets: Have a concise brief, accurate specifications, product images, spokesperson details, demo availability, and clear answers to likely questions ready before pitching.

8. Outreach and Reporting Plan

Before CES

Validate the brief and assets; research and segment reporters/creators; personalize pitches by beat; offer demos or interviews where genuinely useful; and track responses and next steps.

During and after CES

Confirm scheduled demos, coordinate requested materials, respond promptly to follow-up questions, monitor published coverage, verify links and key messages, and summarize learnings.

A simple report would distinguish outreach activity from outcomes. It would track:

  • Number of researched contacts and personalized pitches sent.
  • Replies, positive responses, demo requests, and interviews or briefings scheduled.
  • Coverage published, links, outlet/creator relevance, and which key messages were accurately represented.
  • Referral traffic or other campaign signals only where reliable tracking is available.
  • Lessons for the next outreach round: which angles prompted interest, which questions repeated, and which audiences were a strong fit.

No results are claimed for this exercise. These are the proposed measures for a hypothetical campaign, not a report of outreach that occurred.
