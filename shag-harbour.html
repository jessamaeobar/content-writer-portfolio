The Lights Over Shag Harbour

The 1967 Incident: What the Records Say, and What They Do Not

Opening

On October 4, 1967, people near Shag Harbour, a small coastal community in southwestern Nova Scotia, reported seeing unusual lights moving toward the water. The first official summary described a dark object with four white lights. It said the object dropped quickly, there was a bright flash, and then one light stayed on the surface before disappearing.

Authorities treated the report as a possible aircraft accident. A Coast Guard vessel and local boats searched the area. Canadian military headquarters then requested an underwater investigation. Divers were sent to the reported location, and the search continued over several days.

However, the searches found no identifiable aircraft wreckage or physical evidence to explain the reports.

This is the main documented part of the Shag Harbour incident. Over time, people have retold the story as a UFO crash, an underwater encounter, or proof of a mysterious craft. The surviving records from 1967 support a more cautious account. They show the reports, official actions, and a search that found nothing. They do not prove what the witnesses actually saw.

The story is important because the surviving contemporary records still exist. They show how the report moved from a local sighting to a response by the RCMP, the Coast Guard, and the Canadian military. The paperwork also shows how some details changed or became more certain in later versions of the story.

A Place Called Shag Harbour

Shag Harbour sits on Nova Scotia's Atlantic coast in Shelburne County. The 1967 military records also mention Lower Wood Harbour and Lower Woods Harbour. Later stories usually call it Shag Harbour, but the earliest military documents use these different place names when describing the reported location.

The incident started with reports from people in and around the community. The first official message credits an RCMP corporal from Barrington Passage and six other witnesses. It says the night was clear and there was no moon.

The brief weather note is helpful, but it is not a full observation log. The message does not give detailed visibility or a signed statement from each person. It only summarizes what was sent to the Rescue Co-ordination Centre in Halifax.

The corporal's surname is typed as Wercicky in the first message, although later reproductions do not always render it consistently. The telex does not name the six other witnesses; it says the corporal knew their names. A newspaper report published two days later names several people connected with the incident, but that is a separate source.

This difference is important. An official summary is not the same as having six separate witness statements. It shows what information got to the authorities, but not exactly what each person saw or how their stories might differ.

What the First Official Message Recorded

The earliest key document in the surviving file is a priority message from the Rescue Co-ordination Centre in Halifax to Canadian Forces Headquarters. It was sent on October 5, 1967, and labelled as a UFO report. It gave the sighting date as October 4 and placed the reported object outside Lower Woods Harbour.

According to the summary, the object was dark, more than 60 feet in size, and carried four white lights arranged horizontally, with about 15 feet between them. It moved east at low altitude, then down toward the water. The message estimated the observation lasted more than five minutes, although the exact duration was unknown.

The following part described a rapid descent accompanied by a high whistling sound. It recorded a bright flash when the object reportedly hit the water. A single light reportedly remained on the surface for a long time before sinking, apparently before the RCMP could reach it with a boat.

These details show what was reported to authorities right away. But they are just a summary, not a technical study. The size was only an estimate, and while the object was described as dark, no shape or structure was confirmed. No one had a physical object to measure or inspect.

The telex also recorded a position for the last sighting: approximately 43 degrees, 30.5 minutes north and 65 degrees, 45 minutes west. That coordinate gave searchers a reference point. It should not be treated as the object's precise location on the seabed. The message called it the position of the last sighting, and no object was recovered to establish where it might have gone.

There is another key difference. The report says the object went down to the water and then there was a bright flash. Later reviews of individual accounts show that some people lost sight of the lights behind trees or a hill before seeing a light offshore. Seeing lights move down and then seeing light on the water does not always mean someone watched one object go all the way from the sky into the sea. The official summary keeps the reported version, but it doesn't include each person's full viewing history, so readers can't check every part of the story.

The First Search

Authorities treated the report as a possible aircraft emergency. If a plane had crashed, people might have needed help in the water. This practical concern explains why police, the Coast Guard, and local boats got involved before the story became known as a UFO case.

The October 5 message stated that Coast Guard lifeboat 101 and many small boats searched the area. The result was recorded as “nil results.” The message also said they checked possible leads, including aircraft and flares, but found no explanation.

A later entry said Coast Guard vessel 101 was heading to the area with an RCMP officer to keep searching, using the reported position as a guide. The records call vessel 101 both a “lifeboat” and a “cutter.” The best way to describe it is as the Coast Guard vessel called 101 in the messages.

The first search was an emergency response, not a scientific study. Searchers wanted to find out if an aircraft had crashed and if there were people or debris to find. The fact that boats went out shows the report was taken seriously, but it does not prove what the object was, how big it was, or what it was made of.

A newspaper report published in Halifax on October 7 added a detail that became part of the story: strange foam on the water. It said local fisherman Captain Bradford Shand saw yellow foam. Later stories also said the foam was different from normal tide foam.

This should be seen as a reported observation, not a scientific result. The military records mention the search but include no lab analysis. The sources here do not have a saved sample, a chain-of-custody record, or a chemical test for the foam. Nothing proves it came from an aircraft, fuel, a propulsion system, or any particular object.

Why the Time and Size Are Disputed

Even the exact time of the sighting is inconsistent across contemporary records.

The October 5 Rescue Co-ordination Centre telex gives the time as 0210Z on October 5. The letter Z designates Greenwich time, also known as Zulu time. In 1967, this time reference was expressed as Greenwich Mean Time (GMT). Halifax was observing daylight saving time and was three hours behind Greenwich. Therefore, 0210Z on October 5 corresponds to approximately 11:10 p.m. local time on October 4.

A memo signed by Colonel W. W. Turner, Director of Operations at Canadian Forces Headquarters, lists the time as 2345 hours local on October 4, or 11:45 p.m. This October 6 memo puts the sighting about 35 minutes later than the time in the Rescue Co-ordination Centre message.

The documents do not explain this difference. It could be due to different time information in the reports, a mistake in one summary, or a difference in how the time was recorded. Without an original statement to clarify this, the best approach is to say the event was reported late on October 4, but the two official records list different times.

A similar difference appears in the object's estimated dimensions. The Rescue Co-ordination Centre message states it was over 60 feet, without clearly stating whether that meant length, width, or diameter. Turner's October 6 memorandum describes it as more than 60 feet in diameter. A later memorandum dated October 25 describes it as approximately 60 feet in length.

These descriptions cannot be combined into one exact measurement. They are just estimates repeated in reports, not measurements from a found object. Saying the object was exactly 60 feet long or definitely 60 feet in diameter would make the evidence seem more certain than it really is.

The differences do not make the report meaningless. They show why historical records need close reading. The important point is not to choose the more dramatic number. It is to tell viewers that the surviving contemporary records are not fully consistent.

The Military Investigation Begins

The initial surface search found no aircraft or wreckage. On October 5, Canadian Forces Headquarters asked Maritime Command to investigate the sighting and consider an underwater search. The request was based on the reported position near the coast and the possibility that something had entered the water.

Maritime Command sent a message ordering one diving officer and three men to investigate. It gave the reported position as approximately 43 degrees, 30 minutes north and 65 degrees, 45 minutes west, and described the reported object as about 300 yards offshore.

The divers were told to go to Clark's Harbour, Nova Scotia. At that location, Coast Guard vessel 101 and the RCMP corporal would help them find the exact spot. Investigators had a reference point from the first report and expected a local officer to help confirm it.

The order was operational, not a public announcement that a craft had been found. It asked divers to investigate a reported object. The surviving message does not say the Navy had found anything before the divers arrived. It records the tasking of a team to search; it shows an investigation, not a recovery.

On October 6, Maritime Command directed a thorough search of the area, approved hiring a local boat to continue operations, and indicated that Coast Guard vessel 101 could be released. It also called for periodic situation reports and a final report when the operation was completed.

The instructions outline a clear operation with staff, support, and a reporting process. They do not say what the object was.

The Underwater Search

A message on October 7 arranged for a helicopter airlift of divers and equipment from Canadian Forces Base Shearwater to the Barrington area. The archived message mentions three divers and about 400 pounds of equipment. The detail shows that the investigation required transporting personnel and equipment to the area.

The most revealing search update is dated October 8. It reported that divers had spent two days searching underwater under good conditions. The area described as thoroughly searched measured half a mile by one and a half miles. The message recorded no results.

It also said the underwater search would start again at first light on October 8. If they found nothing by the afternoon, they planned to end the operation. A final message later that day said the search continued but found nothing. The search ended at 2130Z on October 8.

The documents show a more detailed timeline than just saying divers searched for a few days. Divers were told to investigate; the search area grew; two days of underwater work found nothing; the search restarted on October 8 and ended after another attempt with no results.

The messages do not include a detailed dive log for each diver. They do not describe every part of the seabed, the visibility at each depth, or the equipment used each time. They only give updates and the final result. Any account should not make up what divers saw underwater or cite sonar readings or discoveries that are not in the records.

What the Search Did Establish

The search showed that authorities took the report seriously and organized both a surface response and an underwater investigation. It also showed that these searches found no physical evidence to identify the object.

That conclusion appears in a memorandum signed by Colonel Turner on October 25. It was prepared to provide unclassified information in response to a public inquiry from a person in the United States. The memo summarized the October 4 report and the subsequent search.

The October 25 memo said the object was about 60 feet long. Witnesses thought it came down quickly and made a bright splash when it hit the water. The memo also said a single white light stayed on the surface for a while, and the RCMP corporal tried to reach it before it sank.

The memo stated that a search failed to produce material evidence to explain or establish the object's identity. It added that an underwater search by Department of National Defence divers also failed to locate tangible evidence from which to draw a clear conclusion.

This is not simply a later storyteller saying the search failed. It is the wording of a contemporary Department of National Defence summary. The department did not claim to have recovered a craft or identified an object. It reported that the searches found no tangible evidence.

The October 25 memo is just an administrative summary, not a new witness interview or a separate scientific review. It repeats information from earlier reports. The Rescue Co-ordination Centre message and Turner's memos should be seen as related records, not as three separate investigations confirming every detail.

The People Behind the Reports

The first telex counts an RCMP corporal and six other witnesses, but it does not provide a separate account from each person. It says the corporal knew their names. The official message gives a witness total without preserving the individual observations needed to compare what each person saw.

A newspaper report published in Halifax on October 7, 1967, provides additional names and accounts connected with the incident. Later historical research identifies civilians David Kendrick, Norman Smith, and Laurie Wickens, as well as RCMP Constable Ron O'Brien and fisherman Captain Bradford Shand, among the people associated with the sighting and subsequent search. Their accounts and roles were not necessarily the same, and not everyone witnessed the entire sequence.

These people did not necessarily see the same thing. Someone who saw lights in the sky had a different vantage point than an officer who arrived later and saw a light offshore. A fisherman involved in the search could report the water's condition without having watched the earlier aerial observation. Treating everyone mentioned in the article as a witness to the entire sequence would blur those differences.

Later research compared the newspaper story with the official telex and later interviews. Some civilian witnesses said they lost sight of the lights behind trees or a hill. They later saw, or heard about, a light on the water. This gap is important because it means we cannot say for sure that every observer watched one object move from the sky into the sea without interruption.

Laurie Wickens gave a later account in an interview published in 2007, about forty years after the event. His recollection helps explain how one participant remembered the lights and the sequence. It is a retrospective memory, not a statement recorded that night. It can be compared with contemporary reporting, but it should not replace it.

There is also a difference between the number of witnesses and the number of independent accounts. If one person retells the same experience in several interviews, those are several records of one person's recollection, not several independent sightings. The military memoranda also relay information passed through an official reporting chain. They document how the case was handled, but none provides a fresh observation.

The Case in Later Records

The Shag Harbour documents are now part of a bigger collection of Canadian reports on unidentified flying objects. The National Research Council file UAR/N/116–125 includes several 1967 reports, such as those about Shag Harbour. These records are kept by Library and Archives Canada.

Ray MacLeod's October 7, 1967 newspaper report shows how quickly the incident became public news. The headline suggested investigators might find real evidence, but the article said no ships or planes were reported in the area at the time. It showed doubt while the search was still ongoing, not a final answer.

Just because a case is in an archive does not mean it happened exactly as described. Archives keep records an institution made or received, but they do not prove every event happened as reported. In 1967, “unidentified flying object” was just a label for something not yet identified. It described what was seen, not where it came from. Today, the term UAP is used more broadly to describe unidentified aerial or anomalous phenomena. UAP is increasingly used in official and research contexts. UFO remains in common use, particularly when referring to historical cases.

In June 2025, the Office of the Chief Science Advisor of Canada released a report from the Sky Canada Project. The project examined how Canada handles public reports of unidentified aerial phenomena, using archival documents and input from government departments, experts, and other groups.

The report lists Shag Harbour among several well-known Canadian UAP cases. It says witnesses reported a row of lights descending and entering the water, and notes that the RCMP and Coast Guard responded assuming a plane crash, but found no wreckage.

The report is a modern government summary, not a new underwater investigation. Its inclusion of Shag Harbour confirms the case remains part of Canada's public UAP history; it does not confirm a new extraterrestrial object or a military recovery. The October 1967 result remains unchanged: the search failed to identify the object.

What Could the Object Have Been?

The main question is whether the lights came from a regular aircraft, a flare, a reflection, a natural event, or something else. The remaining reports do not provide enough evidence to choose one answer with confidence.

The October 6 Turner memo says the Rescue Co-ordination Centre did a first investigation and discounted an aircraft, flares, floats, or other known objects. This is important to mention, but the memo does not give the technical details behind each decision. It does not include photos, radar data, instrument readings, or a physical object that would let someone today check the findings.

It would therefore be inaccurate to say that all ordinary explanations were scientifically ruled out. It would be equally inaccurate to declare that an extraordinary explanation was proven. The available evidence leaves the case unresolved.

Some later writers, as well as enthusiasts, have suggested secret military activity, an unusual underwater craft, or an object that remained underwater after the search ended. Those ideas are part of the case's popular mythology, but the search messages cited here do not document a recovery or an underwater tracking operation. They describe an investigation that ended after negative results.

A responsible account should not treat missing evidence as proof of a cover-up. Not finding a recovered object does not mean something was hidden. It simply means the searchers did not find evidence to identify what was seen.

The Ending

On October 4, 1967, people near Shag Harbour saw lights coming down toward the water. The first official message described a dark object with four white lights, a quick descent, a flash, and a light that stayed on the surface. Authorities treated the report as a possible aircraft accident.

Coast Guard personnel and local boats searched the area. Canadian military headquarters requested an underwater investigation. Divers searched an area described in the messages as half a mile by one and a half miles. Official updates said the search found nothing, and the operation ended on October 8.

A later Department of National Defence memo said the surface and underwater searches found no solid evidence to identify the object. Canada's 2025 report still lists Shag Harbour as one of its best-known UAP cases, but it offers no new explanation.

These original documents don't agree on the event's exact time and size. Witness accounts also differ on what people saw, including whether they had a clear view of the lights coming down.

What is clear, though limited, is that people reported unusual lights, authorities responded, and searches produced no identifiable wreckage or physical evidence to establish what the object was.

More than fifty years later, the main question is still the same: what were the lights people saw over the water that night?
