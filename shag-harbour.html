THE SHAG HARBOUR INCIDENT

October 4, 1967: The Lights Over Shag Harbour

THE OPENING

On the night of October 4, 1967, people near Shag Harbour, Nova Scotia, reported seeing several bright lights moving down toward the sea.

The authorities were told that an aircraft might have crashed, so Coast Guard crews and local boaters went out to look for survivors and wreckage.

But they found no plane.

The following days brought a much bigger investigation. The Canadian military sent divers to search underwater, hoping to find whatever the witnesses had seen.

They found nothing either.

The incident became one of Canada's best-known UFO cases. But when we look at the original reports and later witness interviews, some important details don't quite match.

So what happened that night? And why couldn't the searchers find anything?

To understand the mystery, let's go back to the first report.

THE FIRST REPORT

Shag Harbour is a small fishing community in Shelburne County, along the southern coast of Nova Scotia.

The original military reports identify the sighting location as outside Lower Woods Harbour, near Shag Harbour.

The first official message came from the Rescue Co-ordination Centre in Halifax. It was sent to Canadian Forces Headquarters on October 5, 1967, the morning after the incident.

The report described a dark object measuring more than 60 feet, with four white lights arranged in a horizontal line. The lights were estimated to be about 15 feet apart.

According to the message, the object was moving east at low altitude before descending rapidly toward the water.

The witnesses reportedly heard a high whistling sound as it came down. Then there was a bright flash, which the report described as happening when the object hit the water.

After that, one white light remained on the surface.

It stayed there for some time before disappearing. The report said it had sunk before the RCMP corporal could reach it by boat.

The observation lasted more than five minutes, although the exact duration was unknown.

The message also described the night as clear, with no moon.

It identified an RCMP corporal from Barrington Passage and listed six other witnesses. Their names were known to the corporal, but the message did not include separate statements from each person.

At this point, the authorities had a reported sighting and a possible aircraft accident. They still had no physical evidence showing what had been seen.

THE FIRST SEARCH

The authorities treated the report as a possible plane crash.

Their immediate concern was whether anyone was in the water and needed rescuing.

Coast Guard lifeboat 101 and several small boats searched the area. But they found no aircraft or wreckage.

The original message recorded the result as "nil results."

The authorities also checked other possible explanations, including aircraft and flares. Those checks produced nothing.

The search continued the following morning. A follow-up message said Coast Guard Cutter 101 was heading to the area with an RCMP officer on board.

Local fishermen were also involved in the search.

One of them, Captain Bradford Shand, described something unusual on the water. A report published in the Halifax Herald on October 7 mentioned patches of bubbling water and foam near the area.

Shand reportedly said he had never seen anything quite like it.

Later interviews recorded a similar observation. Fishermen described patches of foam measuring roughly 30 to 40 yards across and said they did not look like normal tide foam.

But nobody collected a sample for laboratory testing. There was no scientific analysis that could identify what the foam was or connect it to the reported lights.

Still, it was another unusual detail from the search that night.

And the authorities wanted to know whether something was actually lying beneath the water.

THE MILITARY GETS INVOLVED

On October 5, Canadian Forces Headquarters contacted Maritime Command and requested an investigation.

The message specifically asked that an underwater search be considered.

Maritime Command responded by ordering one diving officer and three men to investigate the reported location.

They were instructed to go to Clark's Harbour, where Coast Guard cutter 101 and the RCMP corporal could help them find the exact area.

The military records placed the reported object about 300 yards offshore.

On October 6, Maritime Command ordered the search area to be expanded. It also approved hiring a local boat to help with the operation.

The following day, another message requested a helicopter to transport three divers and about 400 pounds of equipment from Shearwater to Barrington.

The military now had divers, additional equipment, and local boats searching the area.

But they still had no idea what, if anything, they would find underwater.

THE UNDERWATER SEARCH

By October 7, the divers had already spent two days searching the seabed.

A military situation report stated that they had thoroughly searched an area measuring half a mile by one and a half miles.

The conditions were described as good.

Despite that, the divers found nothing.

The plan was to continue searching at first light on October 8. If the team still found no results, the operation would end that afternoon.

The divers returned, but the result remained the same.

A final military message reported that the search continued on October 8, with negative results.

The operation was officially terminated at 2130 Zulu time that evening.

The authorities had responded to the original report, sent out boats, brought in military divers, expanded the search area, and supplied additional equipment.

They had searched a substantial section of the seabed.

But they found no aircraft parts, no wreckage, and nothing that could identify the object.

The underwater search was over.

But there was still another problem with the original reports.

TWO DIFFERENT TIMES

When researchers compare the military documents, they find a difference in one basic detail: the time of the sighting.

The first message from Halifax listed 2:10 a.m. Zulu time on October 5.

Zulu time is the time standard used by military and aviation services. In October 1967, Halifax was three hours behind it.

Converted to local time, the first report places the sighting at 11:10 p.m. on October 4.

However, a memorandum written by Colonel W. W. Turner on October 6 gives the time as 11:45 p.m. local time.

That's a difference of 35 minutes.

The documents don't explain why.

The estimated size also needs some attention.

The first message described the object as more than 60 feet, but did not specify what that measurement referred to.

Turner's October 6 memorandum described it as more than 60 feet in diameter.

The October 25 memorandum described it differently, giving an estimated length of about 60 feet. So the surviving documents don't agree on exactly how large the reported object was or which dimension was being described.

Since nobody recovered an object to measure, there was no way to confirm its actual size.

These differences don't mean the entire report was unreliable. They show that some details were recorded differently as the incident was passed from one office to another.

And the differences become even more interesting when we look at what the witnesses actually saw.

WHAT DID THE WITNESSES ACTUALLY SEE?

The original military summaries describe an object descending into the water.

But a later investigation raised an important question: did anyone actually see the entire descent?

In 1968, the University of Colorado completed a study called Scientific Study of Unidentified Flying Objects. The final report was transmitted to the U.S. Air Force in October 1968 and approved for public release in January 1969. It became known as the Condon Report.

One section, Case 34, examined the Shag Harbour incident.

The investigator, Norman Levine, gathered information through telephone interviews with Canadian military and RCMP personnel who knew about the case.

Interestingly, their accounts didn't agree on how many people first reported the lights. A Navy watch officer said the original report came from two teenagers. An RCMP corporal, however, said it came from five young people between 15 and 20 years old.

According to the corporal, the five were driving near the shore when they saw three or four yellow lights arranged in a horizontal line.

The lights appeared to be descending toward the water at an angle of about 45 degrees.

But then they lost sight of them.

As the young people drove past a small hill, the lights disappeared from view for about ten seconds.

When they could see the area again, they noticed a single white light on the water, near the place where they believed the object had gone down.

They continued driving for about a quarter of a mile before reporting what they had seen to the RCMP.

The gap matters.

The young people saw lights descending toward the water. They later saw a light on the surface. But they didn't have a continuous view of what happened between those two observations.

The Condon Report specifically pointed this out. Its investigator noted that apparently nobody had actually seen anything enter the water.

That doesn't mean the witnesses were lying. It means their accounts did not establish that the lights in the sky and the light on the water were definitely the same object.

The report also described what happened after the young people contacted the police.

Two officers and the corporal arrived about 15 minutes later. They reportedly saw the light still on the water.

It remained visible for about five more minutes before going out.

Ten minutes after the light disappeared, the officers reached the area in a rowboat. A Coast Guard boat and six fishing boats were also there.

They found patches of foam, but no aircraft or wreckage.

The investigation also included accounts from other witnesses.

Two young women driving on an island reportedly saw three yellow lights descending toward the water. They later noticed a yellow light on the surface.

Another witness, about two miles away, saw three red-orange lights moving downward. He also reported hearing a whistling sound, which he compared to a falling bomb.

His view was blocked by houses, and the sound stopped shortly afterward.

These accounts share some similarities, but they aren't identical.

Some witnesses described yellow lights. Others reported red-orange lights. The number of lights also varied.

That makes it difficult to build one complete picture of what happened from the available accounts.

WHAT THE RADAR AND AIRCRAFT CHECKS FOUND

The Condon Report included another interesting detail.

According to the RCMP corporal interviewed by Levine, a nearby RCAF radar station reported no Canadian or American air operations in the area at the time.

The station also reportedly detected no unusual radar object.

The investigation also noted that no missing aircraft had been reported in the area.

These details came from the telephone interviews. The Condon Report did not include the original radar logs, so they cannot be independently examined through that account.

The original military report also said that possible explanations involving aircraft and flares had been checked without results.

Colonel Turner's October 6 memorandum added floats and other known objects to the list.

But the documents don't explain exactly how each possibility was investigated.

So we know the authorities checked several ordinary explanations. What we don't have is enough information to say that every possible explanation was ruled out.

WHAT THE MILITARY CONCLUDED

On October 25, Colonel Turner prepared another memorandum summarizing the incident and the military response.

It repeated the main details of the sighting and the investigation.

The conclusion was straightforward: the searches had failed to produce material evidence that could explain or identify the object.

The underwater investigation had also ended without finding anything that could lead to a clear answer.

The military had taken the reports seriously. It had organized a search, sent divers underwater, and brought in additional equipment.

But the investigation had not identified what the witnesses saw.

The case remained unexplained.

It later became part of Canada's collection of historical UFO reports.

In June 2025, the Office of the Chief Science Advisor of Canada published its Sky Canada Project report. Shag Harbour was included among the country's best-known UAP cases.

The report summarized the 1967 incident, including the reported lights, the emergency response, and the fact that no aircraft wreckage was found.

However, the 2025 report was not a new investigation into Shag Harbour. It examined how Canada handles public reports of unidentified aerial phenomena.

It did not provide new physical evidence or identify the object.

WHAT COULD IT HAVE BEEN?

So, after looking at the original reports, the underwater search, and the later witness interviews, what could the object have been?

There are several possible explanations people might consider, including aircraft, flares, reflections, or natural phenomena.

The authorities checked some of these possibilities in 1967, but the surviving records don't provide enough detail to determine which explanation, if any, fits the observations.

There have also been later claims about secret military activity and an underwater recovery operation.

But the military documents discussed here do not record the recovery of a craft or any material being taken away from the site.

Those claims cannot be treated as established facts.

What we can say is that people reported seeing unusual lights, the authorities investigated, and the search failed to identify what they had seen.

That is where the evidence leaves us.

THE ENDING

On October 4, 1967, people near Shag Harbour reported seeing several lights move down toward the water.

The authorities thought an aircraft might have crashed, so they organized a search.

Coast Guard crews and local boats looked for wreckage. Canadian military divers then searched a large area of the seabed.

They found nothing.

Later investigations also raised questions about what the witnesses actually saw. In particular, the Condon Report noted that apparently nobody had witnessed the object entering the water.

The search ended without recovering anything that could identify what the witnesses had reported.

More than half a century later, the Shag Harbour incident remains unexplained. The records tell us what people saw and what the authorities did to investigate it.

But they still don't tell us what those lights were.
